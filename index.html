Section One

What “mentality” really is

Perception is not a camera. Memory is reconstructive: Frederic Bartlett (1932) showed that people recall stories by reshaping them to fit existing schemas — mental frameworks that organize expectation. Ulric Neisser (1976) later described a perceptual cycle: schemas direct what we sample from the world, and those samples modify the schema in turn. We do not see situations raw; we read them through lenses we did not choose alone.

Daryl Bem’s self-perception theory (1972) adds that we infer our own attitudes partly from watching our own behavior — so repeated action hardens into interpretive habit. Carol Dweck (2006) demonstrated one such lens, mindset: the belief that ability is fixed versus growable shapes how people respond to failure. But mindset is a specific belief about malleability — narrower than mentality as a whole.

Working definition

Mentality is a socially transmitted, culturally patterned interpretive lens — a relatively stable set of assumptions, values, and attribution habits that shapes how a person perceives and reacts to situations. It is not a mood (a transient affective state that passes in hours) and not merely a mindset (one specific belief about ability). It is the grammar of perception a group shares.

Culture writes these lenses. Shared history — colonialism, migration, recurring scarcity — produces shared perceptual habits. A people repeatedly told their culture is inferior will, over generations, often read local products, local accents, and local skin through that inferiorizing frame. A people organized around mutual aid will read a stranger’s trouble as, in some sense, their own. This is why Filipino mentality is not a single thing but a stack: indigenous, colonial, and modern layers, each still active in how a Filipino interprets a promotion, a typhoon, a favor, or a tardy friend. The sections below map that stack — and the quiz that follows lets you observe your own lens at work.

ERA A

Pre-colonial Philippines (pre-1565) — indigenous roots

Framed as largely positive: the perceptual habits that existed before, and survived beneath, colonial rule.

Kapwa

shared inner self · pakikibaka / pakikisama

Positive

The core of Sikolohiyang Pilipino: the self is not bounded but shared — the “outsider” (ibang tao) and “insider” (hindi ibang tao) are poles of one identity. To recognize kapwa is to treat even a stranger as fellow-self.

Enriquez (1994) · Pe-Pua & Protacio-Marcelino (2000)

Bayanihan

communal mutual aid · being heroes together

Positive

Rooted in the barangay practice of carrying a whole house to a new site during rice-harvest season, and in collective farm labor. Work that is crushing alone becomes festive when shared.

Scott (1994, Barangay) · McCoy & Scott on barangay polity

Pakikisama (roots)

smooth interpersonal relations as harmony

Positive

Originally the art of maintaining social accord — “smooth interpersonal relationship” — not mere conformity. Going along is a moral skill of keeping the group whole, later distorted under hierarchy into compliance.

Lynch (1961) · Bulatao (1964)

Babaylan spirituality

healer-leader · harmony with nature & ancestors

Positive

The indigenous healer-leader (often women) who mediated between community, ancestors, and nature — a perceptual frame of harmony and reciprocal relation with the unseen world, suppressed but never erased under Spanish friar rule.

Salazar (1999/2000, pantayong pananaw) · Scott on pre-colonial religion

Barangay communal values

collective welfare over individual · kinship-polity

Positive

The barangay was not a village but a kinship-polity in which welfare was collective and leadership was reciprocal. This layer underlies why Filipinos still read harm to one as harm to the group — and why “pakialam” (involvement) can feel like care rather than intrusion.

Scott (1994) · Jocano (1975)

ERA B

Colonial era & its legacy (1565–1946, continuing) — mostly negative / ambivalent

Three centuries of Spanish rule plus half a century of American tutelage rewrote the lens: several indigenous values were bent toward hierarchy, dependency, and self-doubt. Frantz Fanon (1952/1967) called it the consciousness of the colonized.

Colonial mentality

internalized inferiority · foreign preference

Negative

The internalized belief that Filipino = less: lighter skin better, imported brands better, English more intelligent, foreign validation the only kind that counts. Measured empirically by the Colonial Mentality Scale.

David (2013, Brown Skin, White Minds) · David & Okazaki (2006) · Constantino (1966, The Miseducation of the Filipino) · Fanon (1952/1967)

Hiya

shame · originally protective, distorted under colonialism

Ambivalent

A sense of propriety that protects social standing — but under colonial hierarchy it curdled into fear of authority and ostracism, silencing dissent and enabling compliance with abuse.

Bulatao (1964) · Garchitorena (1979)

Utang na loob

debt of gratitude · reciprocity bent to patronage

Ambivalent

A noble reciprocal bond — gratitude made durable — that colonial and elite patronage systems weaponized into political loyalty: a bag of groceries exchanged for a vote, a job for dynastic obedience.

Hollnsteiner (1961) · Bulatao (1964) · McCoy on patronage dynasties

Bahala na

“come what may” · fatalism vs. deterministic risk-taking

Ambivalent

Debated: either passive resignation under oppression, or “deterministic risk-taking” — active acceptance after doing what one can. Enriquez revalued it as courage amid uncertainty; colonial readings called it laziness.

Lynch (1961) · Bulatao (1964) · Enriquez’s revaluation

Crab mentality

pulling down those who rise · scarcity hostility

Negative

Horizontal hostility: if I cannot rise, neither can you. Often traced to colonial divide-and-rule and real scarcity, where one person’s gain was plausibly another’s loss. Observed widely in popular discourse; Bulatao wrote of envy and social comparison.

Popular discourse · Bulatao on envy / social comparison

Ningas kugon

grass-fire enthusiasm that burns out

Negative

Projects launched in a blaze of excitement, abandoned at the tedious middle. Linked to short-term reward structures, patronage deadlines, and a political culture that rewards the photo-op over follow-through.

Bulatao (1964) · Jocano (1975)

Amor propio

self-esteem / face-saving · sensitivity to criticism

Ambivalent

A heightened regard for self-image that makes criticism feel like personal assault. Protects dignity; can also prevent honest feedback and entrench pride.

Bulatao (1964) · Hollnsteiner (1961)

Filipino time

chronic tardiness · clock-time estrangement

Ambivalent

Often read as rudeness by industrial-clock standards; anthropologically, a polychronic orientation in which relationships and events take priority over the minute hand. Colonial-era estrangement from factory clock-time deepened the gap.

Jocano (1975) · Bulatao (1964)

ERA C

Modern Philippines (contemporary) — mixed valence

The same lenses, refracted through labor migration, social media, recurring disaster, and a consumer market that still sells colonial inferiority as beauty.

OFW / migration success frame

bagong bayani · remittance aspiration

Ambivalent

Migration as national heroism and family strategy: roughly a tenth of the population works abroad, and remittances anchor the economy. Celebrated as bagong bayani (new heroes), yet built on separated families and state dependence on export of labor.

Tyner (2009) · Rodriguez (2010, Migrants for Export) · Asis (2006)

Social-media disinformation perception

troll armies · “alternative facts”

Negative

The world’s social-media capital became a laboratory for networked disinformation. Colonial distrust of local institutions migrated online: established media is suspect, a viral post from a friendly page is trusted.

Ong & Cabañes (2018) · Curato (2017) · Pertierra (2017)

“Pasok na sa banga” survival pragmatism

diskarte · whatever-works resourcefulness

Ambivalent

Ends-justify-means resourcefulness under scarcity: get it into the jar by any route. Adaptive survival when institutions fail; corrosive when it normalizes cutting corners and ignoring rules that apply to others.

E. San Juan Jr. on diskarte / survival pragmatism

“Filipino resilience” discourse

national trait vs. excuse for state failure

Ambivalent

After every typhoon, the headline praises Filipino resilience. Critics counter: resilience-talk can absolve the state of disaster preparedness, turning victims’ endurance into a virtue that lets governments off the hook.

Ty (2014/2015) · Ortega (2020) · Bankoff (2003, Cultures of Disaster)

Revival of kapwa & bayanihan

community pantries 2021 · disaster volunteerism

Positive

When the state faltered in pandemic lockdowns, community pantries (“magbigay ayon sa kakayahan, kumuha ayon sa pangangailangan”) spread spontaneously — indigenous mutual aid reclaimed, not imported. Disaster volunteerism shows the same layer alive.

Claudio (2021/2022) on community pantries · Cayton

Colonial mentality in beauty & consumerism

skin-whitening industry · imported-brand prestige

Negative

The Philippines is one of the world’s largest skin-lightening markets; English remains a class marker; imported brands command a premium. Colonial mentality did not end in 1946 — it became a consumer product line.

David (2013) · Thompson (2010) · skin-lightening market studies

Section Three

The perception check — 50 questions

No question asks “do you have X mentality.” Instead, each presents an everyday scenario and asks which interpretation is closest to your first thought. Your choices reveal which lenses you habitually reach for. You can go back and change answers. Takes about 5–7 minutes.

How do you read the world?

50 everyday scenarios. Four interpretations each. Pick the one closest to your first thought — there are no right answers, only lenses. At the end you’ll see your profile across eight dimensions.
